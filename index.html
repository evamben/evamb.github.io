What if one neighborhood bore the weight of an entire city’s drug crisis?

If one neighborhood carries the weight of an entire city's drug crisis, it’s the Tenderloin. Two decades of data reveal an undeniable truth: while overdose rates fluctuate across San Francisco, the Tenderloin’s numbers keep climbing, a jagged peak towering over the rest. Even at the height of COVID, as seen in article [1], more people in the Tenderloin died from fentanyl than from the virus.

The Mission, the Richmond, Southern? Their numbers barely compare.

If the data makes one thing evident, it’s that drug-related crime in the Tenderloin has been relentless. Incidents have risen steadily over the years, hitting alarming highs in 2008 and 2009, nearly doubling previous figures. In those two years alone, drug-related crimes surged to approximately 9,617, a 60% increase compared to just a few years prior.

More and more crime, yet where was the response? Where was the action to curb this crisis?

In 2010, drug-related crime in Tenderloin finally saw a decline, but the real reduction didn’t occur until January 2014, when the city took one of its first targeted actions: banning parking on Turk Street. This measure was aimed at curbing drug-related activity by preventing dealers and users from congregating in parked cars. Initially, the strategy seemed effective, drug crimes dropped from 1,000 incidents per year and appeared to relax due to the ban. However, this was only a temporary win.

From 2018 onward, crime levels began to rise again, eventually reaching record-breaking levels by July 2022, with numbers recalling the 2008-20010 crisis. Despite the urgency, the government was slow to respond, and it wasn’t until December 2021 that San Francisco declared a state of emergency in the Tenderloin. This included ramping up police patrols and creating more focused operations to tackle drug trafficking and violent crime in the area. The city began working more closely with federal and state agencies to curb the crisis. However, the results were mixed, with drug-related incidents continuing to surge despite increased law enforcement presence.

In May 2023, the DMACC Center (Drug Market Analysis Coordination Center) was launched. The center aimed to streamline efforts by local, state, and federal agencies in combating drug trafficking and organized crime. However, just 29 days later, drug-related incidents surged again, suggesting that either the response time was too long or that the drug crimes in the Tenderloin are relentless.

By November 2023, the numbers were once again at record highs. In response, the government took action, intensifying police patrols in March 2024 along Sixth Street, one of the hardest-hit areas. Although the immediate effects have been minimal, there remains hope for long-term improvement. However, lingering questions remain: Why did the government wait so long to act, and why aren’t the current measures producing the expected results?

All of this is reflected in the time series graph below, which traces the evolution of San Francisco’s drug crisis over time. The data tells a sobering story, while recent years have seen troubling spikes, the long-term trend suggests a slow decline. The crisis is far from over, but perhaps, despite everything, there’s still reason to believe that lasting change is possible.

Mapping the Crime. Where Does It Happen?

Let’s now take a closer look at where crime is actually happening. If we look beyond the numbers and into the map, a new layer of the crisis reveals itself: the geography of drug crime in the Tenderloin has been shifting. Over the years, incidents haven't just increased, they've moved.

{% include drug_incidents_tenderloin.html %}

Figure 3: Drug-Related Incidents by Geolocation and Years

From 2012 onward, we see a noticeable migration of drug-related reports toward the eastern edge of the Tenderloin, especially around Market Square. One detail stands out: this shift aligns almost perfectly with the arrival of Twitter’s headquarters in the area.

In 2010, only 90 drug incidents were recorded near the triangle of Market, Larkin, and McAllister Streets, where Twitter would soon plant its flag. By 2011, that number had already dropped, likely due to increased policing ahead of the company’s move. But once Twitter settled in, the dynamic changed. In 2013, five incidents were reported at 9th and Market Streets—just a two-minute walk from Twitter’s front entrance. The following year, that number increased significantly, with 150 incidents logged in the immediate area. And it didn’t stop there. By 2015, United Nations Plaza, just seven minutes away, recorded 128 drug-related incidents, a massive jump from only 16 in 2011. That’s a 700% increase in four years.

By 2018, crime had edged even closer. One block away from Twitter’s building, 245 incidents were reported. The data paints a clear trajectory: drug-related crime was not just persisting, but concentrating around the headquarters. Then came the pandemic. In 2020, drug crimes dipped—mirroring trends across much of the city. But by 2021, they began rising again. In 2022, the Civic Center area, located just two minutes from Twitter HQ, reported 845 incidents, a staggering figure that dwarfed previous years. Even in 2024, the situation remained tense, with 164 drug-related reports filed right outside the building.

Why did this happen? While it’s difficult to draw a straight line of causation, a convergence of factors likely contributed. Twitter’s arrival marked a turning point in the area’s gentrification, rapidly increasing property values and displacing long-time residents, support services, and community anchors. As economic pressure mounted, vulnerable populations were pushed further to the margins, and street-level crime surged in response. At the same time, the influx of tech workers, visitors, and contractors brought a steady stream of potential targets to the area. Early efforts to police the neighborhood may have temporarily displaced criminal activity, but as the years went on, enforcement fluctuated and the attention faded. What remains is a deeply unequal landscape: a multimillion-dollar tech headquarters surrounded by a growing crisis.
