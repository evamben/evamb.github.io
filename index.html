What if one neighborhood bore the weight of an entire city’s crisis?

What if one neighborhood bore the weight of an entire city’s crisis? Look at the graph: two decades of data, one undeniable truth. While overdose rates fluctuate across San Francisco’s districts, the Tenderloin’s line climbs relentlessly, a jagged peak towering above the rest. Even at COVID’s height,as seen in article [1], more people in Tenerloin died from fentanyl than from the virus.

The Mission, the Richmond, Southern? Their numbers are footnotes in comparison.

The Tenderloin is known as the heart of San Francisco’s inequality. Here, policies fail, health crises outlive emergencies, and progress never lasts. The data doesn’t just hint at this, it shouts it. But there’s a bigger question: Why do things stay so bad here, even when there’s small signs of hope? This is clearest in the area’s long fight against drug crime.

By 2008–2009, drug crimes in the Tenderloin jumped sharply to 9,617, a 60% increase from earlier years. These aren’t just numbers. They show a broken system. Policies meant to fix things often fail when old problems return. Crime dips briefly, then spikes again. The data screams that the Tenderloin is in crisis, but it also reveals a quiet truth: doing nothing can look like doing something. Programs start, money gets spent, yet the streets stay chaotic. So we ask again: When the proof is everywhere and patterns repeat, who’s responsible? When will things actually improve?

More Crime, More Promises. But Where’s the Action?

In 2010, we can observe in the graph below, a decrease in drug-related crimes across the Tenderloin, but the real reduction didn’t occur until January 2014, when the city took one of its first targeted actions: banning parking on Turk Street. This measure was aimed at curbing drug-related activity by preventing dealers and users from congregating in parked cars. Initially, the strategy seemed effective—drug crimes dropped from 1,000 incidents per year and appeared to relax due to the ban. However, this was only a temporary win.

From 2018 onward, crime levels began to rise again, eventually reaching record-breaking levels by July 2022, with figures reminiscent of the 2008-2009 peaks. Despite the urgency, the government was slow to respond, and it wasn’t until December 2021 that San Francisco declared a state of emergency in the Tenderloin. This included ramping up police patrols and creating more focused operations to tackle drug trafficking and violent crime in the area. The city began working more closely with federal and state agencies to curb the crisis. However, the results were mixed, with drug-related incidents continuing to surge despite increased law enforcement presence.

In May 2023, the DMACC Center (Drug Market Analysis Coordination Center) was launched. The center aimed to streamline efforts by local, state, and federal agencies in combating drug trafficking and organized crime. However, just 29 days later, drug-related incidents surged again, suggesting that either the response time was too long or that the drug crimes in the Tenderloin are relentless.

By November 2023, drug-related crimes in San Francisco had reached alarming levels, spiking to heights not seen since 2008-2009. In response, the government took action, intensifying police patrols in March 2024 along Sixth Street, one of the hardest-hit areas. Although the immediate effects have been minimal, there remains hope for long-term improvement. However, lingering questions remain: Why did the government wait so long to act, and why aren’t the current measures producing the expected results?

This situation is clearly reflected in the time series graph above, which tracks drug-related crime trends in the city. Despite recent surges, the overall trend reveals a decline over time. While the recent uptick is troubling, the long-term data shows a positive shift, suggesting that with sustained efforts, the crime rates may continue to fall—offering hope for a better future in areas like the Tenderloin.

Mapping the Crime. Where Does It Happen?

If we look beyond the numbers and into the map, a new layer of the crisis reveals itself: the geography of drug crime in the Tenderloin has been shifting. Over the years, incidents haven't just increased—they've moved. From 2012 onward, we see a noticeable migration of drug-related reports toward the eastern edge of the Tenderloin, especially around Market Square. One detail stands out: this shift aligns almost perfectly with the arrival of Twitter’s headquarters in the area.

In 2010, only 90 drug incidents were recorded near the triangle of Market, Larkin, and McAllister Streets—where Twitter would soon plant its flag. By 2011, that number had already dropped, likely due to increased policing ahead of the company’s move. But once Twitter settled in, the dynamic changed. In 2013, five incidents were reported at 9th and Market Streets—just a two-minute walk from Twitter’s front entrance. The following year, that number increased significantly, with 150 incidents logged in the immediate area. And it didn’t stop there. By 2015, United Nations Plaza—just seven minutes away—recorded 128 drug-related incidents, a massive jump from only 16 in 2011. That’s a 700% increase in four years. By 2018, crime had edged even closer. One block away from Twitter’s building, 245 incidents were reported. The data paints a clear trajectory: drug-related crime was not just persisting, but concentrating around the headquarters. Then came the pandemic. In 2020, drug crimes dipped—mirroring trends across much of the city. But by 2021, they began rising again. In 2022, the Civic Center area, located just two minutes from Twitter HQ, reported 845 incidents—a staggering figure that dwarfed previous years. Even in 2024, the situation remained tense, with 164 drug-related reports filed right outside the building.

Why did this happen? While it’s difficult to draw a straight line of causation, a convergence of factors likely contributed. Twitter’s arrival marked a turning point in the area’s gentrification, rapidly increasing property values and displacing long-time residents, support services, and community anchors. As economic pressure mounted, vulnerable populations were pushed further to the margins, and street-level crime surged in response. At the same time, the influx of tech workers, visitors, and contractors brought a steady stream of potential targets to the area. Early efforts to police the neighborhood may have temporarily displaced criminal activity, but as the years went on, enforcement fluctuated and the attention faded. What remains is a deeply unequal landscape: a multimillion-dollar tech headquarters surrounded by a growing crisis.
